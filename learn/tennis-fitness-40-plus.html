The foundations of tennis fitness after 40

Most players over forty are training in the wrong order. Get the order right and everything else gets easier, including staying on court.

Prefer to watch? The full version is on YouTube. Otherwise, read on.

The short version

  • Mobility comes first. Before strength, before explosivity, before endurance. Stiffness is what turns into injury.
  • Train for victory, not failure. Quality repetitions with intent beat grinding yourself into the ground.
  • Endurance for tennis is not running. Tennis is intermittent, so train the systems that stay clean instead of the one that burns.
  • Recovery is a skill you train. It is not the absence of training, and it is usually the thing limiting you.

There is a moment most players meet somewhere after forty. The tennis is still there. The understanding of the game is better than it has ever been. But the body starts sending bills for things that used to be free.

The usual response is one of two mistakes. Either you keep training the way you did at twenty and collect injuries, or you get cautious, do less, and slowly lose the qualities that let you play at all. Neither is necessary.

What follows is the order I use, and the categories of work that sit inside it. It is simple, but not necessarily easy.

Why the order matters more than the exercises

Most fitness advice for tennis is a pile of exercises with no hierarchy. That is why it fails for older players. You cannot build strength on a body that cannot get into position, and you cannot build endurance on a body that is already breaking down.

So the order is mobility, then strength, then endurance, with recovery running underneath all three. If you only have three hours a week, spend them in that order and you will get more from them than most players get from six.

1. Mobility, the foundation

For players over forty, mobility is not a warm-up. It is the base everything else is built on. Most of us sit far more than we move, sitting makes us stiff, and stiffness is what eventually shows up as an injury.

There are three principles that matter here.

Release your stiffest point daily. Everybody has one. For some players it sits between the shoulder blades, for others around the belt line. Three minutes with a massage ball on that one spot does more than a long generic stretching routine.

Pay attention. Scan your body before you start. Train what is actually sore today rather than what the programme says. And decide how you want to feel when you finish, because that changes what you choose to do.

Do enough repetitions. Not three. Somewhere near the number where you meet your own edge, which for most movements is far more than people expect.

A full routine runs head to toe: neck in three planes, shoulders, band openers, wrists, some rope work, rotations, then hips, which are non-negotiable, then knees over toes on a slant board, then squats, which are also non-negotiable, then adductors, calves, and the feet. It takes fifteen minutes.

The best practice is the one that you actually do.

2. Strength, or building your armour

Strength work after forty is not about looking like an athlete on Instagram. It is about building armour, so the body can take what the sport asks of it.

Five boring things decide most of your result, before any programme matters at all. Sleep, eight hours or more. Enough protein. Enough calories to actually build something. Low enough stress, because stress and your capacity to recover pull from the same place. And a proven plan, rather than the newest method you saw this week.

Then one principle that changes everything: train for victory, not for failure. Grinding every set until you collapse teaches your body to fail. Quality repetitions, performed with intent, stopping while the movement is still good, teach it to succeed. This matters more with every year you add.

A caution specific to tennis. Mass for its own sake can cost you the sport. I worked with a player who put on serious size and then found he had no shots left that fired. Strength has to serve the game, not compete with it.

3. Endurance, which is not running

This is where I made my worst mistake, so I will use myself as the example. As a teenager I asked a professional how to build endurance for tennis. He said one word: running. So I ran. An hour forty-five one day, two and a half hours the next. I tore my Achilles.

Worse than that, it did not even work. There was a player I regularly beat in fitness testing who kept outlasting me on court. Fit on paper, gassed in play. That contradiction took me years to understand.

Tennis is an intermittent sport. Six seconds of work, twenty seconds of rest, repeated for two hours. Your body has systems for that, and one system that fights it.

  • The short, powerful system. Clean, efficient, and exactly what a point demands.
  • The aerobic system. Clean, efficient, and what carries you across two hours.
  • The burn. Wasteful and expensive. This is the one to minimise, not the one to chase.

Train the first two well enough and you rarely fall into the third. That is the whole idea. Practically it looks like matches themselves, slow steady aerobic work where you can still breathe through your nose, short explosive repeats of six to ten seconds with real rest between them, and breath work. Not long painful runs.

The rest intervals are not padding, by the way. After a genuinely hard six-second effort, full clearance can take considerably longer than feels reasonable. Cutting rest short is how you end up training the burn you were trying to avoid.

4. Recovery, the one that decides the rest

If you want to keep improving after forty, become good at recovering. It is not the absence of training. It is a skill, and it is usually the thing actually limiting you.

Wave your load. Your body can sustain roughly two hard weeks in a month, not four. The load has to rise and fall rather than climb forever, and the players who ignore this are the ones who break in month three.

Understand that you do not improve while you are stressed. You improve once your body switches into its rest-and-digest state and does the repair work. A lot of recovery is simply making that switch happen on purpose.

And measure rather than guess. A two-minute heart-rate variability test in the morning turns "I feel alright today" into something you can actually train against.

One thing that surprises people: rest is not always stillness. I coached a player who collapsed in a final after a full afternoon of complete rest. His system needed light activation, not silence. Once we added short easy work on those days, his recovery improved measurably. What works depends on who you are.

Where to start this week

Do not rebuild everything. Pick the mobility routine, do it four times this week, and pay attention to what your body says afterwards. That single change is usually enough to make the next thing possible.

Then add one strength session where you stop every set while the movement still looks good. Then, and only then, worry about endurance.
